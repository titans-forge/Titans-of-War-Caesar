# Historical Sources and Scenario Boundaries

## Bibracte, 58 BC

- Julius Caesar, *Commentarii de Bello Gallico*, Book I.
- The game models Caesar's reported use of successive lines and the later Boii and Tulingi flank threat.
- Three legionary lines are compressed into three front-line cohorts and one player-controlled reserve. Timings, distances, and formation counts are gameplay abstractions.

## The Sabis, 57 BC

- Julius Caesar, *Commentarii de Bello Gallico*, Book II, especially sections 16-28. Public translations are available through [Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.02.0001%3Abook%3D2) and [LacusCurtius](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Caesar/Gallic_War/2%2A.html).
- The scenario follows Caesar's sequence: concealed approach, attack during camp preparation, Roman success on the left, crisis around the Twelfth and Seventh, Caesar's rally, and arrival of the rear column.
- "Sambre" is retained only where discussing the traditional English name. Modern location arguments frequently identify the ancient Sabis with the Selle; the game title therefore uses "The Sabis."
- Cohort-sized formations stand in for larger legionary elements. Caesar's casualty and enemy-strength figures are not treated as exact simulation inputs.

## Gergovia, 52 BC

- Julius Caesar, *Commentarii de Bello Gallico*, Book VII, especially sections 44-53. Public translations are available through [Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.02.0001%3Abook%3D7) and [LacusCurtius](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Caesar/Gallic_War/7D%2A.html).
- The scenario follows Caesar's distinction between a limited opportunity and a general battle: deception draws defenders away, Roman troops take lower positions, some continue too far, the recall fails to stop the advance, and an uphill counterattack produces a Roman reverse.
- Caesar assigns much of the failure to troops exceeding their orders. Because he is explaining his own defeat, the game treats that apportionment of responsibility as a claim rather than a neutral verdict.
- Two lower camps, four Roman cohorts, the recall threshold, distances, and timings are teaching abstractions. The scenario does not claim to reconstruct the disputed battlefield geometry exactly.

## Alesia, 52 BC

- Julius Caesar, *Commentarii de Bello Gallico*, Book VII, especially sections 69-90. Public translations are available through [Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.02.0001%3Abook%3D7) and [LacusCurtius](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Caesar/Gallic_War/7G%2A.html).
- The scenario models the inward circumvallation, outward contravallation, simultaneous attacks from the garrison and relief army, and the final pressure against works disadvantaged by the terrain.
- Five Roman formations and five attacking groups compress a much larger operation into readable command sectors. The three waves and breach threshold are gameplay abstractions.
- Caesar's reported totals for the relief army are not used as exact simulation inputs. His account magnifies the scale of the victory and remains the surviving commander's political narrative.

## Pharsalus, 48 BC

- Julius Caesar, *Commentarii de Bello Civili*, Book III, especially sections 84-99. Public translations are available through [Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.02.0076%3Abook%3D3) and [LacusCurtius](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Caesar/Civil_Wars/3D%2A.html).
- The scenario follows Caesar's reported sequence: Pompey masses cavalry, archers, and slingers against Caesar's right; Caesar forms a fourth line from cohorts withdrawn from the third; the reserve drives off the cavalry; and those cohorts continue into Pompey's exposed left while Caesar's fresh third line advances.
- Four Caesarian formations and five Pompeian formations compress armies that Caesar reports in tens of thousands. The cavalry arrival, penetration threshold, reserve path, and victory conditions are gameplay abstractions.
- Caesar presents himself as anticipating Pompey's plan and identifies his fourth line as the origin of victory. That coherent command narrative is useful for the scenario, but it is also the victor's self-presentation in a civil conflict and not a neutral staff record.

## Alexandria, 48 BC

- Julius Caesar, *Commentarii de Bello Civili*, Book III, especially sections 106-112. A public translation is available through [LacusCurtius](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Caesar/Civil_Wars/3D%2A.html).
- The anonymous *Bellum Alexandrinum*, especially sections 1-33, continues the Roman operational account. A public translation is available through [LacusCurtius](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Caesar/Alexandrian_War/A%2A.html).
- The scenario follows the opening strategic problem: Caesar holds a restricted palace quarter with a small force, fighting spreads through streets and the harbor, the Alexandrian fleet threatens Roman communications, and occupation of Pharos preserves access for supplies and reinforcements.
- Three urban strongpoints, one Pharos column, a single causeway garrison, and a timed royal counterattack compress a prolonged and confused urban war. The terrain is a readable historical reconstruction, not a claim to reproduce the exact shoreline or palace-quarter street plan.
- Caesar's narrative ends as the Alexandrian war begins, and the continuation is by an uncertain author writing from the Roman side. The famous fire's extent and damage are not represented as settled facts.

## Zela, 47 BC

- The anonymous *Bellum Alexandrinum*, sections 65-78, is the principal tactical account. A public translation is available through [LacusCurtius](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Caesar/Alexandrian_War/E%2A.html).
- The scenario follows its sequence: Caesar takes a ridge opposite Pharnaces, begins a camp, leaves only a front line under arms, is surprised by an attack across the ravine, recalls the working parties, stops scythed chariots with missiles, wins first on the Sixth Legion's wing, and storms the royal camp.
- Plutarch, *Life of Caesar* 50, connects the campaign's speed with the later phrase *veni, vidi, vici*. The game uses that phrase only for a successful after-action result rather than placing invented dialogue in Caesar's mouth during battle.
- Four Roman formations, four Pontic combat groups, the reaction window, and the staged path to the camp are teaching abstractions. The terrain preserves the opposing ridges and ravine but does not claim exact archaeological placement.
- The author writes from Caesar's side and attributes a prominent role to divine favor. The scenario instead grades observable command transitions: formation, absorption of shock, counterattack, and exploitation.

## Beyond Carrhae, 44 BC alternate history

- Suetonius, *Julius Caesar* 44.3, reports that Caesar intended to deal first with the Dacians and then campaign against Parthia through Lesser Armenia, testing Parthian strength before risking a major battle. [Public translation](https://lexundria.com/suet_jul/44.3/r)
- Appian, *Civil Wars* 2.110, records preparations for a long campaign against the Getae and Parthians. [Public translation](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Appian/Civil_Wars/2%2A.html)
- Plutarch, *Life of Caesar* 58, preserves a much grander projected itinerary. It is useful evidence for Caesar's later reputation and ambitions but not a reliable operational plan for this hypothetical battle.
- The scenario's Armenian-border battlefield, opposing formations, timing, and result are invented. Horse archery, cataphract shock, protected supply, and the danger of pursuit are historically grounded command problems assembled into a teaching exercise.
- The game deliberately defines victory as route security and preservation. It does not assert that Caesar would have conquered Parthia or that one tactical success could settle the Roman-Parthian contest.

Caesar is both the principal surviving military witness and a political author describing his own command. The historical campaign uses his account while keeping its self-presentational character visible. The Parthian epilogue is labeled alternate history wherever it appears.
